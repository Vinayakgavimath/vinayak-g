The problem

Missing moments, not missing effort

Freshers apply to multiple roles across platforms. But tracking is inconsistent or doesn't exist at all. I faced this myself. I noted things in a notebook, forgot to update, and eventually lost track completely. The tracking became more exhausting than the actual job search.

Most people end up with a half-filled spreadsheet, a few notes, and a lot of anxiety about what they might have missed.

"Everything just disappears after applying. I have no idea what's happening."
Fresher, actively job searching
"I got a call from HR and wasn't prepared at all. I didn't even remember the role."
Recent graduate
"I use a spreadsheet but I forget to update it after a while."
Engineering student
"Follow-up emails feel important but I never know when to send them."
BCA student
Research insight

The problem wasn't a missing app. It was unsustainable effort.

I spoke to 5 students and freshers actively applying, asking them to walk me through their last week of job searching. Not what tools they used. What actually happened. Three things came up every single time.

01

Tracking exists, but breaks down

Almost everyone had some system: spreadsheet, notes app, notebook. None of them kept it up past the first week. Consistency was the actual problem.

02

Applications scatter across platforms

LinkedIn, Naukri, company websites, referrals through WhatsApp. No single place showed the full picture.

03

Missed moments happen after applying, not during

The applying part was fine. The follow-up, the status check, the interview prep. That's where things fell apart.

The problem wasn't a lack of tools. It was that every tool required effort people couldn't keep up. Any system that depends on consistency will eventually break.

Design principles

Three principles every decision was tested against

01

Remind without depending on memory

The system notifies at the right moment so users don't have to remember follow-ups or deadlines themselves.

02

Always-visible status

Users should instantly know where every application stands without digging through tools or scrolling through a list.

03

Effortless to maintain

Logging an application or updating a status had to take under 10 seconds. If it feels like work, people stop.

Design decisions

Every screen decision traced back to research

The annotated breakdowns below show not just what I designed, but why each decision was made and what it was solving for.

Screen 01 Today's Actions — Home Dashboard
Problem it solves

"I don't know what needs attention right now"

Users told me they'd open tracking apps, see a flat list of 15 applications, feel overwhelmed, and close the app without doing anything.

Showing a full list on the home screen felt overwhelming in wireframe testing. Nobody wants to scan 15 cards to figure out what to do. So I pulled only the urgent stuff to the top. One or two things, max. Everything else stays one tap away but out of the way.

Why this layout

Status cards over a table or list

I tested a table layout in wireframes. Users couldn't read it without squinting on mobile. Cards gave each application enough breathing room to be scanned, not read.

I tried a table layout first. On mobile it was unreadable. Cards gave each application enough room to actually be scanned. The status badge is the first thing your eye hits and that's on purpose. You should know the state of an application before you read the company name.

Screen 02 Quick Status Update — Bottom Sheet
Problem it solves

"Updating my tracker feels like extra work"

Every user I interviewed had abandoned their tracking system at some point. The common reason: updating it took too many steps. It felt like a second job.

A separate edit screen means navigation, a back button, losing your place. A bottom sheet slides up, you tap, it's done. No context switch. I timed it with the 5 users from research and everyone completed the update without being told what to do. That was the bar I was aiming for.

Why no form fields

Tap-to-select, not type-to-update

An earlier version had a text field for notes. Every user skipped it. When I removed it and made status selection the only action, completion rate went up.

Early version had a notes field. Every user skipped it. Once I removed it and made status the only action, people actually finished the flow. Tapping a button has no commitment anxiety. Filling a text field does. That's the whole reason this screen exists the way it does.

Screen 03 Reminders — Today / Tomorrow / Upcoming
Problem it solves

"I miss follow-up windows. I don't know when to act."

Users knew follow-ups mattered. They just never knew when the right moment was. Generic app notifications had trained them to ignore alerts.

I tried a calendar view first. Too much to look at, and users had to interpret dates before they could act. Today / Tomorrow / Upcoming does the interpretation for them. Today creates urgency. Tomorrow creates prep. Upcoming is just awareness. Nobody has to think about what the date means.

Why not push notifications alone

A screen you want to open, not just a ping

Push notifications get ignored. I tested this assumption early. Users said they'd turned off notifications for most apps. The reminders screen had to be useful enough that users would open it proactively, not just when pinged.

Most users said they had notifications turned off for basically everything. So I couldn't rely on pings. The reminders screen needed to be worth opening on its own. Something you'd check in the morning before opening LinkedIn. Reliable enough to become a habit.

Screen 04 Application Detail — Status + Next Action
Problem it solves

"I know I applied. I don't know what to do next."

One user told me: "I always know the company name. I never know what step I'm on." That single insight shaped this entire screen.

One user put it best: "I know I applied, I just don't know what to do next." So instead of just showing status, I added a suggested next step based on where the application is. Applied? Send a follow-up in 5 days. Interview scheduled? Start prepping questions. You don't have to figure it out yourself.

Why a timeline view

History, not just current status

Status alone tells you where you are. A timeline tells you how you got here and gives context for what comes next.

In testing, nobody could tell me when they last followed up with a company. They'd just forgotten. The timeline is there so you can walk into an interview knowing you've had 3 touchpoints with that team. That kind of context actually matters when you're nervous and trying to remember everything.

Screens 05 – 06 Add Application + Onboarding
Add Application
3 required fields only. Company, role, and source. Everything else is optional and can be filled later.
Onboarding (V2)
Reduced from 3 screens to 1 after testing showed users dropped off before reaching the home screen. One value prop. One CTA.
All Applications
Full list is secondary, not the home screen. Users go here when they want overview, not to figure out what to do today.

First version had 8 fields. Job link, salary, contact name, notes. All 5 users hesitated on the form. One said it felt like "filling out a form to fill out a form." I cut it to 3: company, role, where you applied. Everything else optional, added later. That change alone removed all hesitation in testing.

Iteration

The decision I almost got wrong

During prototype testing, one user said something that stopped me: "The onboarding is too much. By the time I get to the app, I've already lost interest." I had built a 3-screen onboarding that explained every feature. It felt thorough. It was actually a wall.

V2 change

Onboarding collapsed from 3 screens to 1

I cut the feature explanations entirely. One screen, one sentence about what the app does, one CTA. Users reach the home screen in 2 taps instead of 5. Three of the 5 test users said the new onboarding felt like the app trusted them. That was the shift I was going for: stop explaining, just let people in.

This was a good reminder that the features you're most proud of are often the ones users care about least. Users don't want to learn an app. They want to use it.

Process

How I built it

01

User interviews

Spoke to 5 students and freshers. Asked them to walk me through their last week of job searching, not what tools they used but what actually happened. Patterns were immediate and consistent across all 5.

02

Problem reframing

My first instinct was to build a better tracker. Research kept pointing to the same thing: the problem was consistency, not the tracker itself. I shifted focus to reducing effort required, not improving how it tracked things. That single reframe changed every subsequent design decision.

03

User flow mapping

Mapped three core flows before wireframing: adding an application, receiving a reminder, updating a status. Each had a time target of under 15 seconds. Any step that broke that limit got cut or simplified.

04

Wireframes to high-fidelity

Low-fi focused purely on information hierarchy and layout logic. High-fi introduced the design system: large tap targets, bottom sheets for quick actions, clear status color coding, and a type scale that works at glance speed.

05

Prototype testing + V2

Tested with the same 5 users from research. Measured task completion time for logging an application and finding what needed attention today. Onboarding feedback from one user triggered the V2 redesign. That ended up being the single most impactful change in the whole project.

What I learned

Solving the right problem matters more than solving it well

The right problem isn't always the obvious one

I started designing a better tracker. Research showed the problem was consistency, not tracking. That one reframe changed everything about how the product works.

Small decisions change behavior

Reducing the status update from a form to a bottom sheet didn't feel like a big design decision. But users in testing said it was the reason they'd actually keep using it.

Reminders are not a solution

Every existing tool already had reminders. Users were ignoring them. The real solution was surfacing the right action at the right moment, not firing more notifications.

If it feels like work, people stop

The 10-second rule for status updates came from watching users abandon tasks mid-flow during testing. Friction doesn't just slow people down. It stops them entirely.
