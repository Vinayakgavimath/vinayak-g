The problem

Missing moments, not missing effort

Freshers apply to multiple roles across platforms. But tracking is inconsistent or doesn't exist at all. I faced this myself. I noted things in a notebook, forgot to update, and eventually lost track completely. The tracking became more exhausting than the actual job search.

Most people end up with a half-filled spreadsheet, a few notes, and a lot of anxiety about what they might have missed.

"Everything just disappears after applying. I have no idea what's happening."
Fresher, actively job searching
"I got a call from HR and wasn't prepared at all. I didn't even remember the role."
Recent graduate
"I use a spreadsheet but I forget to update it after a while."
Engineering student
"Follow-up emails feel important but I never know when to send them."
BCA student
Research insight

The problem wasn't a missing app. It was unsustainable effort.

I spoke to 5 students and freshers actively applying, asking them to walk me through their last week of job searching. Not what tools they used. What actually happened. Three things came up every single time.

01

Tracking exists, but breaks down

Almost everyone had some system: spreadsheet, notes app, notebook. None of them kept it up past the first week. Consistency was the actual problem.

02

Applications scatter across platforms

LinkedIn, Naukri, company websites, referrals through WhatsApp. No single place showed the full picture.

03

Missed moments happen after applying, not during

The applying part was fine. The follow-up, the status check, the interview prep. That's where things fell apart.

The problem wasn't a lack of tools. It was that every tool I found required effort people couldn't keep up. Any system that depends on consistency will eventually break. People aren't consistent.

Design principles

Three principles every decision was tested against

01

Remind without depending on memory

The system notifies at the right moment so users don't have to remember follow-ups or deadlines themselves.

02

Always-visible status

Users should instantly know where every application stands without digging through tools or scrolling through a list.

03

Effortless to maintain

Logging an application or updating a status had to take under 10 seconds. If it feels like work, people stop.

Key screens

Every screen mapped to a real failure point

Problem → "I don't know what needs attention"

Today's Actions

Surfaces time-sensitive tasks like follow-ups and upcoming interviews at the top. The most urgent action is impossible to miss the moment you open the app.

Problem → "I miss follow-up deadlines"

Smart reminders

Reminders organized as Today, Tomorrow, and Upcoming. Specific nudges at the right time, not a generic notification you eventually learn to ignore.

Problem → "Updating feels like extra work"

Quick status update

A bottom sheet that lets you change application status in under 10 seconds. No navigation, no forms. Just tap and done. Designed to remove the friction that makes people stop tracking.

Problem → "I don't know what to do next"

Application detail with next action

Each application shows its current status and the next step to take. Not just where you are. Where you need to go next.

Process

How I built it

01

User interviews

Spoke to 5 students and freshers. Asked them to walk me through their last week of job searching, not what tools they used but what actually happened. Patterns were immediate.

02

Reframing the problem

My first instinct was to build a better tracker. But research kept pointing to the same thing: the problem was consistency, not the tracker itself. So I shifted focus to reducing how much effort the app required, not improving how it tracked things.

03

User flow mapping

Mapped three key flows: adding an application, receiving a reminder, updating a status. Made sure each was self-contained and fast before moving to wireframes.

04

Wireframes to high-fidelity

Low-fi focused purely on information hierarchy. High-fi added a design system built around minimal input: large tap targets, bottom sheets for quick actions, clear status indicators.

05

Prototype testing

Tested with the same 5 users from research. Measured task completion time for logging an application and finding what needed attention today. Both hit target under 15 seconds.

What I learned

Solving the right problem matters more than solving it well

The right problem isn't always the obvious one

I started designing a better tracker. Research showed the problem was consistency, not tracking. That one reframe changed everything about how the product works.

Small decisions change behavior

Reducing the status update from a form to a bottom sheet didn't feel like a big design decision. But users in testing said it was the reason they'd actually keep using it.

Reminders are not a solution

Every existing tool already had reminders. Users were ignoring them. The real solution was surfacing the right action at the right moment, not firing more notifications.

If it feels like work, people stop

The 10-second rule for status updates came from watching users abandon tasks mid-flow during testing. Friction doesn't just slow people down. It stops them entirely.
