Priority

Most task apps fail not because they're missing features, but because tasks go invisible after you add them. I kept missing my own deadlines because of this. So I designed an app around one question: what if the task just stayed in front of you?

The problem

Tasks don't get forgotten. They become invisible.

I built this because I kept missing my own deadlines. Not because I forgot. Because I'd add a task, close the app, and never see it again unless I deliberately went looking for it.

I noticed the same thing with other students. Didn't matter which app they used: Notion, Google Tasks, a calendar, sticky notes. The problem kept showing up. You add the task. Then it disappears into a list. Then you miss it.

The apps weren't broken. The visibility was.

Research insight

The answer was hiding in sticky notes

A few students I talked to had gone back to physical sticky notes. That surprised me at first. But once I sat with it, it made complete sense.

A sticky note on your desk doesn't go anywhere. You can't swipe it away or close the tab. You don't need to remember to open it. It just sits there until the task is actually done.

That's when I stopped thinking about better notifications and started thinking about presence. The task needs to live somewhere you already look, not somewhere you have to go.

The fix wasn't a smarter reminder. It was just keeping the task somewhere you'd see it anyway, without having to do anything.

01

Reminders don't stick

You see them, tap dismiss, and they're gone. If I'd built a smarter notification system, I'd have solved the wrong problem. Students weren't missing reminders. They were ignoring them.

02

A long task list doesn't help you decide

Students with full Notion pages and long to-do lists weren't getting more done. If anything, seeing everything at once made it harder to start. Too much choice, not enough direction.

03

It has to work without the user doing anything

The moment the user needs to open an app to check their tasks, you've already lost half of them. Visibility has to be automatic, not something they opt into.

Key screens

One priority task. Always in front of you.

Everything in the product connects back to one thing: the task needs to be somewhere you already look. The dashboard, the lock screen, the home screen widget. Wherever your eyes go during the day, the task is there.

Core screen

One task, front and center

Instead of a full task list, the dashboard shows one thing: your priority for today. Everything else is still there, but it doesn't compete for attention. I spent a lot of time on this screen getting the hierarchy right. If it shows too much, it becomes another list to ignore.

Always visible

Lock screen and home screen widget

The priority task shows up on the lock screen and as a home screen widget. You see it without opening the app. That's the whole point. It has to be there whether you look for it or not.

Motivation layer

Streak and progress tracking

Completing your priority task builds a streak. A few students in testing said the streak was the main reason they kept opening the app. It gave them a small sense of momentum that a flat task list never did. I almost cut it as unnecessary. Glad I didn't.

Version 2

When testing broke my assumption

I showed the prototype to the same 6 students from earlier. Most of it landed well. The single task focus made sense to them. The widget idea made sense.

But one student said something I wasn't ready for:

"What if the task itself is stressing me out? I don't want to see it all day."

That broke something I hadn't even thought to question. I'd assumed that more visibility was always better. This showed me it isn't. Sometimes the task is the source of the anxiety, and staring at it all day makes things worse, not better.

So I went back and designed a snooze feature for V2. Not to hide the task permanently, just to give some breathing room. You step back for a bit. It comes back when you're ready. That one comment was probably the most valuable thing that came out of testing the whole project.

V2 additions

What changed after testing

Three things I designed for the next version based on what I heard from real users.

Snooze option that hides the task temporarily without deleting it or losing your streak
Breathing mode: a 5-minute focus break that pauses visibility without breaking the flow
Customizable widget intensity: choose how prominent the task appears on your lock screen
Process

How I built it

01

Started with my own frustration

I kept missing deadlines despite having a task app. That was the starting point. I wanted to understand if other students had the same problem before assuming it was just me.

02

6 student interviews, behavioral focus

Asked each person to walk me through their last week. Not what apps they use, but what actually happened with their tasks. The sticky note pattern came up in 4 out of 6 conversations.

03

Mapping the core behavioral loop

Mapped three moments: adding a task, staying aware of it, completing it. Almost scrapped this step. Glad I didn't. It caught two screens I didn't need and simplified the whole flow.

04

Low-fidelity wireframes

First version had too much on screen. I stripped it back until only the priority task dominated. The rule I kept returning to: if everything is highlighted, nothing is.

05

Design system and high-fidelity

Built a simple system: 90% neutral, 10% accent. The visual language had to support clarity, not add noise. Took three attempts to get the widget placement right.

06

Prototype testing and V2

Tested with the same 6 users. Most feedback confirmed the direction. One piece of feedback fundamentally changed it. That led to the snooze feature and a second round of design.

What I learned

I almost built the wrong product

Research corrected my first instinct

I went into this thinking I'd build a smarter reminder system. But students were already tuning reminders out. I would have shipped something that looked good and solved nothing. Talking to people first saved me from that.

One clear principle makes every decision easier

"The task should be there without you looking for it." That one sentence filtered every feature decision. If it didn't support that, it didn't go in. Having a principle like that is genuinely useful, not just something you write in a case study.

Your best assumptions will still be wrong

I was confident visibility was the answer. One student in testing showed me it's more complicated than that. The snooze feature only exists because I was wrong about something I was sure of. Test early.

Simple products require more thinking, not less

One task on screen sounds like the easy option. But it took the most work to get right. Which task? What if the user ignores it? What happens after it's done? Simple to use doesn't mean simple to design.
