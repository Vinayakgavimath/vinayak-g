The problem

Tasks don't get forgotten. They become invisible.

I built this because I kept missing my own deadlines. Not because I forgot. Because I'd add a task, close the app, and never see it again unless I deliberately went looking for it.

I noticed the same thing with other students. Didn't matter which app they used: Notion, Google Tasks, a calendar, sticky notes. The problem kept showing up. You add the task. Then it disappears into a list. Then you miss it.

The apps weren't broken. The visibility was.

"I write it down and then never look at it again."

"Reminders just become noise after a while. I swipe them away."

Research insight

The answer was hiding in sticky notes

A few students I talked to had gone back to physical sticky notes. That surprised me at first. But once I sat with it, it made complete sense.

A sticky note on your desk doesn't go anywhere. You can't swipe it away or close the tab. It just sits there until the task is actually done. That's when I stopped thinking about better notifications and started thinking about presence.

The fix wasn't a smarter reminder. It was just keeping the task somewhere you'd see it anyway, without having to do anything.

01

Reminders don't stick

You see them, tap dismiss, and they're gone. Students weren't missing reminders. They were ignoring them. Building a smarter notification system would have solved the wrong problem entirely.

02

A long task list doesn't help you decide

Students with full Notion pages weren't getting more done. Seeing everything at once made it harder to start. Too much choice, not enough direction.

03

It has to work without the user doing anything

The moment the user needs to open an app to check their tasks, you've already lost half of them. Visibility has to be automatic, not something they opt into.

Design decisions

Every screen decision traced back to research

The annotated breakdowns below show not just what I designed, but why each decision was made and what it was trying to solve.

Screen 01 Dashboard: One task, front and center
Problem it solves

"I open the app and don't know where to start"

Every student I talked to had too many tasks competing for attention. The result was decision paralysis. They'd open the app, look at the list, and close it without doing anything.

I spent the most time on this screen. The principle was simple: show one thing. Your priority for today. Everything else is still there but hidden unless you ask for it. If two tasks compete for the top spot, the screen breaks down. So I designed it so you pick one task each day, deliberately, and that's what you see. No negotiation once it's set.

Visual hierarchy

If everything is highlighted, nothing is

Earlier versions had the task name, due date, category, priority level, and a progress bar all visible at once. It looked complete. It was overwhelming.

I stripped the dashboard back to three things: the task name, a single action button, and the streak. That's it. Every element I removed made the task itself more visible. The rule I kept coming back to: if adding something doesn't make the priority clearer, cut it.

Screen 02 Lock Screen and Home Screen Widget
Problem it solves

"I have to remember to open the app"

The core insight from sticky notes was that you don't go looking for them. They're just there. That's exactly what the lock screen and widget were designed to replicate.

The widget and lock screen integration aren't extra features. They're the whole point. The task has to exist somewhere you already look, not somewhere you have to go. Your phone lock screen is something you see 50 to 100 times a day without trying. Putting the task there removes any dependency on memory or habit.

Why not just notifications

Notifications get dismissed. Widgets don't.

Three of the 6 users I interviewed had push notifications turned off for most apps. They'd trained themselves to ignore pings. A widget is persistent. It doesn't go away when you swipe it.

This was the key distinction I kept coming back to. Notifications are interruptive and dismissible. Widgets are ambient and persistent. One asks for your attention. The other just exists in your peripheral vision until the task is done. That's a completely different relationship with the user.

Screen 03 Streak and Progress Tracking
Problem it solves

Completing one task doesn't feel like progress

A few students mentioned that task apps gave them no sense of momentum. You complete something, it disappears, and the list just gets shorter. There's no feeling of moving forward.

I almost cut the streak entirely. It felt like a gimmick. But in testing, 4 out of 6 users said it was the main reason they kept coming back. Not the task itself, the streak. It turns a single daily action into a chain you don't want to break. That's behavioral design that actually works, not just decoration.

Why it sits on its own screen

Kept separate from the task on purpose

I didn't put the streak counter on the main dashboard because I didn't want users thinking about the streak while they were trying to focus on the task.

Progress is a reward, not a distraction. You go to the progress screen after completing your task, not before. That order matters. If the streak is always in your face, it shifts the motivation from doing the work to protecting the number. Those are different things.

Screen 04 Add Task
Problem it solves

Adding a task should take less thought than doing it

If capturing a task takes more effort than it feels worth, people just don't do it. They keep it in their head instead, which is worse.

The add task screen has one required field: what is the task. Everything else, time, category, notes, is optional and collapsed. The assumption I designed around was that most tasks don't need metadata. They just need to be captured before the thought disappears. You can always add more detail later.

Why only one task at a time

You can add more. Only one shows on the dashboard.

Some users worried they couldn't add a backlog of tasks. They can. But only the one they mark as today's priority appears on the dashboard and widget.

This was the question that came up most in testing: "What if I have more than one task?" You can see all of them in the all tasks view. But the dashboard forces a choice. Which one matters most today? That question is the product. Making users answer it once a day is the whole behavioral design.

Screen 05 Task Detail and Snooze (V2)
What broke my assumption

"What if the task itself is stressing me out?"

One student in testing said something I hadn't prepared for. She didn't want to see the task all day because it was making her anxious, not motivated. I'd assumed more visibility was always better. That was wrong.

That single comment sent me back to redesign. I added a snooze option: hide the task for a set time without deleting it or breaking your streak. The streak pauses, not resets. You're not punished for needing a break, just gently nudged when you're ready. That one user saved this feature from never existing.

Why snooze and not delete

Deleting a task is admitting defeat. Snoozing isn't.

Psychologically those are very different actions. Deleting a task removes it permanently. Snoozing just moves it forward. Users are much more willing to snooze than delete something they actually want to do.

I designed three snooze options: 1 hour, tomorrow morning, and this weekend. These aren't arbitrary. They map to real mental states. "1 hour" means I need a break. "Tomorrow" means today isn't the day. "Weekend" means this is a project task, not a daily one. Each option respects a different relationship with the task.

Version 2

When testing broke my assumption

I showed the prototype to the same 6 students from earlier. Most of it landed well. The single task focus made sense. The widget idea made sense.

But one student said something I wasn't ready for:

"What if the task itself is stressing me out? I don't want to see it all day."

That broke something I hadn't thought to question. I'd assumed more visibility was always better. This showed me it isn't. Sometimes the task is the source of the anxiety, and staring at it all day makes things worse.

So I went back and designed a snooze feature. Not to hide the task permanently, just to give some breathing room. That one comment was probably the most valuable thing that came out of testing the whole project.

V2 additions

What changed after testing

Three things I designed for the next version based on real user feedback.

Snooze option that hides the task temporarily without deleting it or resetting your streak
Breathing mode: a short focus break that pauses visibility without breaking the flow
Customisable widget intensity: choose how prominent the task appears on your lock screen
Process

How I built it

01

Started with my own frustration

I kept missing deadlines despite having a task app. I wanted to know if other students had the same problem before assuming it was just me.

02

6 student interviews, behavioral focus

Asked each person to walk me through their last week. Not what apps they use, but what actually happened with their tasks. The sticky note pattern came up in 4 out of 6 conversations.

03

Mapping the core behavioral loop

Mapped three moments: adding a task, staying aware of it, completing it. Almost skipped this step. Glad I didn't. It caught two screens I didn't need and simplified the whole flow.

04

Low-fidelity wireframes

First version had too much on screen. I stripped it back until only the priority task dominated. The rule I kept returning to: if everything is highlighted, nothing is.

05

Design system and high-fidelity

Built a simple system: 90% neutral, 10% accent. The visual language had to support clarity, not add noise. Took three attempts to get the widget placement right.

06

Prototype testing and V2

Tested with the same 6 users. Most feedback confirmed the direction. One piece of feedback fundamentally changed it. That led to the snooze feature and a second round of design.

What I learned

I almost built the wrong product

Research corrected my first instinct

I went in thinking I'd build a smarter reminder system. But students were already tuning reminders out. I would have shipped something that looked good and solved nothing. Talking to people first saved me from that.

One clear principle makes every decision easier

"The task should be there without you looking for it." That sentence filtered every feature decision. If it didn't support that, it didn't go in. Having a real principle like that is useful beyond case studies.

Your best assumptions will still be wrong

I was confident visibility was the answer. One student in testing showed me it's more complicated than that. The snooze feature only exists because I was wrong about something I was sure of. Test early.

Simple products require more thinking, not less

One task on screen sounds easy. But it took the most work to get right. Which task? What if the user ignores it? What happens after it's done? Simple to use doesn't mean simple to design.
