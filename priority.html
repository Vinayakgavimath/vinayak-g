The Problem

Tasks don't get forgotten. They become invisible.

I built this because I kept missing my own deadlines. Not because I forgot. Because my tasks were invisible after I added them.

I would add something to my task manager and it would just sit there. I wouldn't see it again unless I actively opened the app and scrolled through my list. That doesn't work. You can't rely on someone to remember to check.

I noticed the same thing happening with other students. They used different tools - Notion, Google Tasks, Calendar, sticky notes but faced the same problem. The apps were there, but out of sight.

  • Tasks get buried under other items
  • No clear signal about what matters most
  • Reminders come and go, then you start ignoring them
  • You can't see progress until you actively open the app

Some students actually switched back to sticky notes. Sounds odd, but it made sense. Sticky notes stayed on your desk. You couldn't ignore them. You didn't have to remember to check.

The problem was not task creation. The problem was task visibility.

Research

What I learned from students

I talked to 6 students. I didn't ask "what app do you use?" I asked "walk me through your last week." Same pattern every time.

"I write it down and then never look at it again."

Arjun, engineering student

"I use sticky notes because at least I can see them."

Sneha, BCA student

"Reminders just become noise after a while. I swipe them away."

Rohan, fresher

The pattern was clear. Digital tools were failing because they hide tasks after you add them. Reminders are temporary — you dismiss them and they're gone. Apps require you to remember to open them. But sticky notes sit there. Visible. Impossible to miss.

Key Insight

Make it impossible to ignore

Sticky notes work because they stay visible. You don't have to remember to check them. That's the entire idea behind Priority — make the most important task impossible to ignore, without depending on the user to remember.

Core Finding

Most task apps bet on reminders. But reminders are a temporary jolt — you see them, dismiss them, and they vanish. The real solution is not a better reminder. It is persistent visibility in the places where students already live.

This insight became the foundation for everything. Instead of building a smarter notification system, I focused on keeping the task visible passively — without requiring any action from the user.

Solution

One priority task. Always visible.

Instead of managing multiple tasks equally, the system highlights one priority task at a time. The experience brings the most important task to the forefront and keeps it visible through home screen widgets and the lock screen.

Single priority task focus Lock screen widget visibility Progress streak tracking

This removes dependency on memory. The task stays visible wherever you look. You don't have to remember to check. It's just there. The goal was a calm, focused experience that helps users complete what truly matters — not manage a list.

Design Process

How I built it

1

Defining the Core Flow

Mapped the core user journey across three key moments: adding a task, staying aware of the task, and completing the task. This ensured visibility and clarity throughout, not just on the dashboard.

I almost skipped this step. Glad I didn't. It caught two screens I didn't need.

Core visibility flow — illustrating the behavioral loop from task awareness to completion

2

Low-Fidelity Wireframes

Created wireframes to prioritize the most important task, reduce visual clutter, and create a calm focused layout. This allowed quick structural validation before any visual decisions.

First version had too much on screen. I stripped it back until only the priority task was dominant.

3

Design System

Defined a simple design system including colors, typography, and spacing. The goal was a visual language that supports clarity rather than distraction. 90% neutral, 10% accent — intentional and minimal.

I kept coming back to one rule: if everything is blue, nothing is blue.

4

High-Fidelity Design

Translated wireframes into final screens focused on strong visual hierarchy, clear priority indication, minimal interface, and consistent interaction patterns across dashboard, task creation, widgets, and onboarding.

Took three attempts to get the widget placement right. The lock screen version only clicked after I spent a week actually using a widget myself.

Validation

What students said

I showed the prototype to the same students from research and asked them to walk through it.

Most immediately got it — one priority task instead of overwhelming lists. The constant visibility through widgets made sense for reducing forgetting. But one student said something that stuck with me:

"What if the task itself is stressing me out? I don't want to see it all day."

That broke my initial assumption that visibility is always good. It led me to add a snooze option in V2 — not to hide the task, but to let users breathe. You can step back for a bit. The task doesn't disappear. It just gives you space when you need it.

The core idea works. But even good ideas need flexibility.

Outcome

What I delivered

I designed a complete mobile task app from scratch. User flow, wireframes, design system, and a clickable Figma prototype. But what I am more proud of is the thinking behind it. I started with the wrong solution and research corrected me. The final product is simpler than my first draft because I kept removing things that did not serve the core idea.

Learnings

What I learned

I almost built the wrong product

My first instinct was a smarter reminder system. But talking to students showed the problem was not reminders — they were already ignoring those. The real fix was passive visibility. That only came from research, not assumptions.

A strong design principle acts like a filter

"One priority task, always visible" made every decision easier. If a feature didn't serve that idea, it didn't belong. That's more useful than a long list of design rules.

Good assumptions break when you test them

I assumed visibility was always positive. One piece of feedback from a real user broke that. The snooze feature exists because I was wrong, and I'm glad I found out before shipping.
