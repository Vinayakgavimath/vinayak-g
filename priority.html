The Problem

Tasks don't get forgotten. They become invisible.

I built this because I kept missing my own deadlines. Not because I forgot — because my tasks were invisible after I added them.

I'd add something to my task manager and it would just sit there. I wouldn't see it again unless I actively opened the app and scrolled through my list. That doesn't work. You can't rely on someone to remember to check.

I noticed the same thing happening with other students. They used different tools — Notion, Google Tasks, Calendar, sticky notes — but faced the same problem. The apps were there, but out of sight.

  • Tasks get buried under other items
  • No clear signal about what matters most
  • Reminders come and go. Then you ignore them
  • You can't see progress until you open the app

Some students actually switched to sticky notes. Sounds weird for a designer to say, but it made sense — sticky notes stayed on your desk. You couldn't ignore them. You didn't have to remember to check.

The problem wasn't task creation. The problem was task visibility.

Research

What I learned from students

I talked to 6 students. I didn't ask "what app do you use?" — I asked "walk me through your last week." Same pattern every time.

"I write it down and then never look at it again."

— Arjun, engineering student

"I use sticky notes because at least I can see them."

— Sneha, BCA student

"Reminders just become noise after a while. I swipe them away."

— Rohan, fresher

The pattern was clear. Digital tools were failing because they hide tasks. Reminders are temporary — you dismiss them and they're gone. Apps require you to remember to open them. But sticky notes? They sit there. Visible. Impossible to miss.

Key Insight

Make it impossible to ignore

Sticky notes work because they stay visible. You don't have to remember to check them. That's the entire idea behind Priority — make the most important task impossible to ignore, without depending on the user to remember.

Most task apps bet on reminders and notifications. But reminders are a temporary jolt. You see them, dismiss them, and they vanish. Over time you stop even looking at them.

The real solution isn't a better reminder. It's persistent visibility in the places where students already live.

Solution

One priority task. Always visible.

Instead of managing multiple tasks equally, the system highlights one priority task at a time. Rather than presenting long task lists, the experience brings the most important task to the forefront.

Single priority task focus Lock screen widget visibility Progress streak tracking

This approach removes dependency on memory. The task stays visible on the lock screen, in the widget, wherever you look. You don't have to remember to check. It's just there.

Design Process

How I built it

1

Defining the Core Flow

Mapped the core user journey across three key moments: adding a task, staying aware of the task, and completing the task. This ensured visibility and clarity throughout, not just on the dashboard.

Low-Fidelity Wireframes

Created wireframes to prioritize the most important task, reduce visual clutter, and create a calm focused layout. This allowed quick structural validation before visual design.

Design System

Defined a simple design system including colors, typography, and spacing. The goal was a visual language that supports clarity rather than distraction. 90% neutral, 10% accent — intentional and minimal.

Validation

What students said

I showed the prototype to a few students and asked them to walk through it.

Most immediately got it — one priority task instead of overwhelming lists. The constant visibility through widgets made sense for reducing forgetting. But one student said something that stuck with me: "What if the task itself is stressing me out? I don't want to see it all day." That broke my initial assumption that visibility is always good. It led me to add a snooze option in V2 — not to hide the task, but to let users breathe. You can step back for a bit. The task doesn't disappear. It just gives you space when you need it.

The core idea works. But even good ideas need flexibility.

Outcome

What I delivered

  • A complete mobile task app concept solving visibility rather than adding complexity
  • User flow mapping the full behavioral loop from awareness to completion
  • Low and high fidelity designs across dashboard, widget, task creation, and onboarding
  • A defined design system with color tokens, typography, and spacing
  • A clickable Figma prototype simulating the core product experience
Learnings

What I learned

The biggest thing I learned: I almost built the wrong product. My first instinct was a smarter reminder system. But talking to students showed the problem wasn't reminders — they were ignoring those already. The real fix was passive visibility. That only came from research, not assumptions.

I also learned that a strong design principle acts like a filter. "One priority task, always visible" made every decision easier. If a feature didn't serve that idea, it didn't belong. That's way more useful than a long list of design rules.
