The problem

Students are drowning in tools and still falling behind

Talk to any college student and you hear the same story. Assignments land in WhatsApp groups. Notes live in Google Docs. Deadlines get announced in class and forgotten by evening. Attendance tracked somewhere in a university portal nobody visits until it's too late.

The result isn't laziness. It's a system that was never designed to work together. Students spend more energy figuring out what the work is than actually doing it.

The real problem isn't productivity. It's visibility. And the mental cost of not having it.

Research insights

What students actually said

Before touching Figma, I spoke to 3-5 students and asked them to walk me through their last week. Not what apps they used. What actually happened. The patterns were consistent across everyone.

01

3 to 5 tools switched daily just to stay on track

Switching between 3 to 5 tools daily adds up. Every jump costs mental energy. Students weren't disorganized. The system was.

02

Deadlines don't get forgotten. They lose visibility.

A WhatsApp message gets buried. A notebook page gets skipped. A Google Doc isn't opened again. The information exists. It just becomes impossible to find at the right moment.

03

No one knows where they actually stand

Without a unified view, students can't accurately assess their progress. They either overestimate how on track they are, or feel overwhelmed without knowing why.

Process

How I built it

01

Problem definition through student conversations

Asked students to walk me through their last week, not tell me what apps they use. That one change in framing exposed the real problem: it wasn't missing features, it was a broken workflow.

02

User flow mapping

Mapped four key flows: viewing assignments, creating tasks, tracking progress, monitoring attendance. Made sure they connected into a single system, not isolated screens.

03

Low-fidelity wireframes

Built wireframes focused on layout, information hierarchy, and flow. No visual decisions at this stage. Structure first.

04

High-fidelity design

Designed 8 screens with a consistent design system. Key visual principle: hierarchy over decoration. High priority items read urgent at a glance, not after scanning.

05

Prototype and concept feedback

Shared the clickable prototype with students from research. The dashboard kept coming up as the most valuable part. Not because it does something new, but because it removes the need to open 4 different things just to figure out what's due today.

What I learned

Simplifying complexity is the hardest design skill

Start with behavior, not features

The most useful design decisions came from understanding how students actually behave. Mapping real tools they use made every subsequent choice more grounded.

Reducing friction is a feature

The biggest UX wins weren't additions. Every time I cut a layer of navigation or consolidated two actions into one, the product became more usable.

Visual hierarchy is product strategy

Deciding what to show first isn't really a visual choice. It's a product decision. Getting the hierarchy right meant students could spot what needed attention without having to scan the whole screen.

Design for clarity over completeness

It's easy to add features. It's much harder to resist. Every simplification required actively choosing what to leave out. The most impactful moment was removing something redundant, not adding something new.
