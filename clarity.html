The problem

Students are drowning in tools and still falling behind

Talk to any college student and you hear the same story. Assignments land in WhatsApp groups. Notes live in Google Docs. Deadlines get announced in class and forgotten by evening. Attendance tracked somewhere in a portal nobody opens until it's too late.

The result isn't laziness. It's a system that was never designed to work together. Students spend more energy figuring out what the work is than actually doing it.

The real problem isn't productivity. It's visibility. And the mental cost of not having it.

Research insights

What students actually said

Before touching Figma, I spoke to 5 students and asked them to walk me through their last week. Not what apps they used. What actually happened. The patterns were consistent across everyone I talked to.

01

3 to 5 tools switched daily just to stay on track

Every context switch costs mental energy. Students weren't disorganized. The system forced them to be scattered. No single place had the full picture.

02

Deadlines don't get forgotten. They lose visibility.

A WhatsApp message gets buried under 200 other messages. A notebook page gets skipped. A Google Doc isn't opened again. The information exists. It just becomes impossible to find at the right moment.

03

Nobody knows where they actually stand

Without a single view, students can't accurately assess their own progress. They either overestimate how on track they are, or feel overwhelmed without knowing why.

Design decisions

Every screen decision traced back to research

The annotated breakdowns below show not just what I designed, but the reasoning behind each key decision.

Screen 01 Dashboard
Problem it solves

"I have to open 4 different things just to know what's due today"

Every student I interviewed described the same morning routine: open WhatsApp, then Docs, then the university portal, then maybe a notebook. That's four context switches before you've done any actual work.

The dashboard was designed to answer one question the moment you open it: what do I need to do today? Assignments due within 24 hours are at the top, visually distinct from everything else. You don't scroll to find them. You don't filter. They're just there. The hierarchy does the work so you don't have to.

Visual hierarchy decision

Urgency is shown, not labeled

An earlier version used a red "Urgent" tag next to deadline items. In wireframe review I realised that made it feel like a warning system, which was anxious rather than helpful.

I shifted to visual weight instead of labels. Urgent items are larger, higher contrast, and positioned first. The visual system communicates priority without adding noise. You know what to do without being told to panic about it.

Screen 02 Assignment Management
Problem it solves

"I know the assignment exists. I just can't find it when I need it."

Students told me they'd screenshot assignment details from WhatsApp or copy them into random notes. The information lived in 3 places but was useful in none of them at the right moment.

The assignment management screen keeps everything in one sortable, filterable list with status always visible. Submitted, pending, overdue. You see the full picture at once. No switching tabs, no scrolling through chat. I designed status as a column, not a tag, because columns are scannable and tags require reading.

Why no calendar view

A list is faster to scan than a calendar when you have 10 deadlines

I considered a calendar layout. Students with multiple subjects and overlapping deadlines would spend more time interpreting the calendar than acting on it.

A list sorted by due date gives you the same information faster. You see the next 5 deadlines immediately without having to navigate weeks or interpret a grid. Calendar view is a nice-to-have for planning. A deadline list is what you need when you're already stressed about what's due.

Screen 03 Progress Tracking
Problem it solves

"I feel behind but I don't actually know how behind I am"

Three students mentioned a specific anxiety: not knowing if they were actually keeping up or just feeling like they weren't. Those are very different situations that feel identical without data.

Progress tracking exists to replace vague anxiety with a real number. Completion percentage per subject, overall standing, tasks done this week. When you can see you're at 70% on three subjects and 40% on one, you know exactly where to focus. The screen doesn't motivate through streaks or celebration. It just shows the truth clearly.

Why per-subject breakdown

Overall progress hides the problem that actually needs attention

A single overall progress bar would look healthy even if one subject was critically behind. Students needed granularity, not a summary that masked the issue.

I designed subject-level bars instead of one aggregate number. The point is to surface imbalance, not to make you feel good about average performance. If you're at 90% in four subjects and 20% in one, the aggregate would show 74% and you'd feel fine. The per-subject view shows you the problem immediately.

Screen 04 Attendance Monitoring
Problem it solves

"I only check attendance when it's already too late to fix it"

Two students told me they'd discovered they were below the required attendance threshold right before exams. Not because they weren't paying attention. Because there was no easy way to check until they went looking for it.

The attendance screen shows your current percentage per subject alongside the minimum required. The gap between the two is visible at a glance. If you're at 78% and the minimum is 75%, you know you have some buffer. If you're at 74%, you see the problem before it becomes a crisis. Proactive visibility instead of reactive panic.

Why it's on the dashboard too

Attendance below threshold should be impossible to miss

Keeping attendance isolated to its own screen meant students would only see it if they went looking. That's the same problem we're trying to solve everywhere else.

If a subject drops below the required threshold, it surfaces on the main dashboard alongside assignments. You don't need to navigate to the attendance screen to know there's a problem. The dashboard tells you. This was a deliberate product decision: some information is too important to stay in its own section.

Process

How I built it

01

Problem definition through student conversations

Asked students to walk me through their last week, not tell me what apps they use. That framing exposed the real problem: it wasn't missing features, it was a broken workflow that nobody had designed end to end.

02

Mapping the actual tool stack

Before wireframing, I listed every tool each student used and where information lived. WhatsApp, Google Docs, university portals, notebooks, phone calendars. Seeing it all together made it obvious why things fell through the cracks.

03

User flow mapping

Mapped four key flows: viewing assignments, tracking progress, monitoring attendance, and getting a daily overview. The goal was to make sure they connected into one coherent system rather than isolated screens.

04

Low-fidelity wireframes across all 8 screens

Wireframes focused purely on information hierarchy and layout. No color, no type choices, no icons. Just: what goes where, and in what order. This step caught two redundant screens before I ever opened a color picker.

05

Design system and high-fidelity

Built a single consistent system before touching individual screens. Type scale, color roles, spacing rules, component library. Having the system in place meant every screen felt like it came from the same product.

06

Prototype and feedback

Shared the clickable prototype with the same students from research. The dashboard kept coming up as the most valuable part. Not because it does something new, but because it removed the need to open 4 different things just to figure out what's due today.

What I learned

Simplifying complexity is the hardest design skill

Start with behavior, not features

The most useful design decisions came from understanding how students actually behave, not what they said they wanted. Mapping real tools and real workflows made every subsequent choice more grounded.

Reducing friction is a feature

The biggest wins weren't additions. Every time I cut a layer of navigation or consolidated two actions into one, the product became more usable. Simplicity is the result of many small decisions to remove things.

Visual hierarchy is product strategy

Deciding what to show first isn't really a visual choice. It's a product decision. Getting the hierarchy right meant students could spot what needed attention without scanning the whole screen.

Design for clarity over completeness

It's easy to keep adding. Much harder to resist. Every simplification required actively choosing what to leave out. The most impactful moment in this project was removing something redundant, not adding something new.
